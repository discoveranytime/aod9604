AOD9604
the fat-loss fragment

A modified slice of human growth hormone — engineered to keep hGH’s fat-burning trick while ditching its growth effects. It went through real clinical trials for obesity, didn’t make the cut. Today it lives on as a research chemical with a loud forum fanbase. Here is the fragment, separated from the hype.

Quick read

AOD9604 is a modified version of growth hormone’s 176–191 fragment (with an added tyrosine for stability) — the tail end of hGH that researchers linked to fat breakdown without the growth effects like raised blood sugar or IGF-1. An Australian company took it through phase 2 obesity trials; it didn’t progress to approval. Later research pivoted to cartilage repair. It is sold online only as an unregulated research chemical.

01The fragment

What AOD9604 actually is

AOD9604 is a lab-modified fragment of human growth hormone — the 176–191 tail, stabilized with an extra tyrosine — designed to trigger fat breakdown without hGH’s growth effects. It’s the rare gray-market peptide with a real clinical-trial past.

The whole short chain is the product: growth hormone’s tail, tweaked for stability — fat signal in, growth signal out.

A slice of growth hormone

Full hGH does many things — grows tissue, raises blood sugar, lifts IGF-1. Researchers found its tail-end fragment seemed to trigger fat breakdown on its own.

Modified for the job

The natural 176–191 fragment was tweaked — a tyrosine added for stability — creating AOD9604: a purpose-built fragment, not a natural hormone.

Not hGH, not a shortcut to it

AOD9604 doesn’t meaningfully raise IGF-1 or mimic full growth hormone. Whatever it does, it does without hGH’s growth machinery — which was the entire design goal.

What’s in the name: Anti-Obesity Drug, compound 9604 — built from growth hormone’s 176–191 tail. A catalog label, not a promise.
Plain English

Imagine growth hormone as a Swiss Army knife. Scientists snapped off the fat-burning blade, sharpened it, and threw away the rest of the knife. AOD9604 is that blade — useful-looking, but it never passed inspection as a real tool.

02The trial story

A real drug program that didn’t make it

Unlike most research chemicals, AOD9604 has a documented clinical history: development, phase 2 obesity trials, a cartilage pivot — and no approval at the end of the road.

A genuine drug-development arc — that ended without a drug.

A real company, real trials

An Australian firm developed AOD9604 and ran phase 1 and phase 2 trials for obesity. This isn’t forum lore — it’s a documented clinical program that didn’t advance.

The pivot to cartilage

Later research explored AOD9604 for cartilage repair and osteoarthritis — a second act that also never reached approval. Two indications, zero approvals.

Plain English

Most gray-market peptides never got near a clinic. AOD9604 did — real company, real trials, real volunteers — and the program still stalled. That history is both its best credential and its honest verdict: tested, then shelved.

“Lower belly fat finally started moving after months of plateau.”

What people report: People describe the change as slow and specific — the belt notch, not the scale. Many say it took two to three months before they noticed anything, and almost everyone pairs the story with diet or training changes they were already making. A recurring detail: the fat that “moved” was the soft kind around the waist, not deep visceral fat. Skeptics in the same threads point out that anyone dieting for three months usually breaks a plateau eventually, with or without a peptide. No growth sides

“No bloat, no blood sugar weirdness like GH.”

What people report: Users comparing it to growth hormone often list what they didn’t feel: no swollen hands, no numbness, no morning blood sugar spikes. For people who had rough experiences with GH or GH boosters, this absence is the selling point. But several careful voices note that not feeling side effects is not the same as not having them — silent changes wouldn’t be noticed without bloodwork. A few report getting labs done and finding nothing alarming, though those are self-reported numbers from strangers. The “clean” reputation rests mostly on the absence of complaints rather than the presence of safety data.

Caveat: absence of noticed effects isn’t a safety study — or proof of the intended effect.
Pattern: “clean” framed as “working.”

06Risks & unknowns

Risks & unknowns

A discontinued drug program doesn’t equal a safe gray-market product. The risks of the vial are a separate file from the risks of the trials — here’s both, in boxes.

Observed

Unregulated vials

Observed fact: what’s sold as AOD9604 has no quality oversight. Gray-market peptide testing repeatedly finds impurities, wrong doses, or substituted compounds.

Observed

The program was discontinued

Observed fact: the obesity development program was not advanced after phase 2. Whatever the full reasons, the developer walked away.

Theoretical

Fragment ≠ inert

Theoretical: “just a fragment” doesn’t mean biologically quiet — fragments can have off-target effects the parent hormone doesn’t. Never systematically studied here.

Unknown

Long-term effects

Unknown: no chronic-use safety data in humans. What prolonged fragment exposure does is uncharted.

Unknown

Interactions

Unknown: no drug-interaction studies, no data in diabetes, pregnancy, or metabolic disease — the exact populations drawn to a fat-loss peptide.

Combinations to be cautious about

Theoretical only — no human interaction studies exist for AOD-9604, with anything. What follows is mechanistic speculation: educated guesses about where overlaps could cause trouble, based on how these substances work — not evidence, not advice, and not a prediction about you. Anyone taking medication should discuss it with a clinician, full stop.

Other GH-axis peptides

Stacking the fragment with GH boosters (CJC-1295, ipamorelin, tesamorelin, MK-677, HGH) mixes two opposite strategies — dodging the GH axis and riding it. The fragment was designed to avoid GH/IGF-1 effects; adding GH-axis drugs reintroduces exactly the machinery it was built to sidestep, plus combined effects nobody has studied. The A-vs-B chapter shows how different these approaches are — combining them doesn’t combine their safety profiles.

Insulin & diabetes drugs

Full growth hormone raises blood sugar and opposes insulin; the fragment was designed not to. But “designed not to” isn’t “proven not to” — and anyone managing blood sugar with insulin, metformin, or GLP-1 drugs is tinkering with the exact metabolic system these peptides touch. This is the highest-stakes overlap on purely theoretical grounds, and the population most drawn to a fat-loss peptide is the one with the least safety data.

Stimulants & thyroid meds

Forum stacks love pairing the fragment with clen-style stimulants or thyroid hormones for cutting. Each stresses the cardiovascular system and metabolism on its own; combined, the strain adds up in ways no trial has ever measured. The anecdotes credit the stack for the results — the risks stack just as silently. When everything is unregulated and unstudied, “no reported problems” means no one is watching, not that nothing happens.

Why this is guesswork

Drug interactions are discovered in studies and pharmacovigilance systems. AOD9604 has neither — no interaction trials, no safety monitoring, no adverse-event database. Everything above is reasoning from mechanisms, which is how hypotheses start, not how safety is established. The honest summary: we don’t know what it interacts with, because nobody has looked.

Plain English

Known: unregulated vials and a discontinued program. Theoretical: fragments can surprise you. Unknown: chronic safety and interactions. The trial history answers “does it work well enough” — it doesn’t answer “is the vial safe.”

The scienceAOD-9604 is a synthetic peptide modeled on the 176–191 amino acid region of human growth hormone, with an added tyrosine residue to improve stability.
